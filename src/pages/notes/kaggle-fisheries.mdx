## The goal: how likely is each species in every picture?

### Submission and evaluation

Submissions are evaluated with the **multi-class logarithmic loss**. Each image has been labelled with **one** true
class, and for each image you submit a predicted probability for every class. The formula is

<Formula display tex={String.raw`\text{logloss} = -\frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N} \sum_{j=1}^{M} y_{ij} \log\left(p_{ij}\right)`} />

### Target categories

Eight categories are available: **albacore tuna, bigeye tuna, yellowfin tuna, mahi-mahi, opah, sharks, other** (there
are fish, but not of the above kinds) and **no fish** (no fish in the picture).

### What makes it hard

**Imbalanced fish types.** Table 1 lists the number of images per type. For the detection pipeline and the
classification pipeline alike, it is crucial to have a roughly balanced number of images per type — otherwise the
network ends up biased towards certain fish (albacore, here) and the cute moonfish gets biased against.

**Varied lighting.** Day and night shots from the boats’ cameras look very different — more on that
[below](#day-night).

**Similar species — albacore vs bigeye** ([photos](https://web.archive.org/web/20190105015541/http://fishidentificationblog.blogspot.com/p/tuna-species.html)):

- **Albacore tuna:** an extremely long pectoral (side) fin that can reach past the first dorsal fin, up to the anal fin
  near the tail; a very dark upper body; a bright silver belly without vertical or horizontal lines; black finlets
  between the second dorsal fin and the tail.
- **Bigeye tuna:** the pectoral fin can pass the first dorsal fin, but is not as long as the albacore’s; a round body
  with vertical lines (adults) and stretch marks on the belly; a big head with big eyes; yellow finlets with a black
  border.

### What makes it easier

**One fish per image.** Each image contains only one fish category — apart from the occasional very small fish used as
bait.

## The pipeline

### 1. Fish detection

**Collecting images.** Thanks to the collective effort of Kagglers, we had
[rectangular bounding-box](https://www.kaggle.com/c/the-nature-conservancy-fisheries-monitoring/forums/t/25902/complete-bounding-box-annotation)
and [mouth-and-tail](https://www.kaggle.com/c/the-nature-conservancy-fisheries-monitoring/forums/t/25565/annotated-mouth-and-tail)
annotations from the very beginning, made with the annotation tool [sloth](https://github.com/cvhciKIT/sloth). Because
different people annotated, the mouth and tail can fall outside the rectangular box. For every annotated fish I also
extract around ten background patches of the same size that do not overlap the fish.

<Figure
  src={[gtDemo, headTail]}
  alt={[
    'The sloth annotation tool showing a boat-deck photo with a red bounding box around a fish.',
    'The same photo with the fish’s mouth and tail marked as two points.',
  ]}
>
  Annotations for the shark in img_01307. Left: rectangular bounding box. Right: mouth-and-tail annotation.
</Figure>

**Training a bottleneck CNN.** I went back to the good old HOG + SVM sliding-window approach from pedestrian detection:
collect positive fish images and negative background (boat) images, following
[Building powerful image classification models using very little data](https://blog.keras.io/building-powerful-image-classification-models-using-very-little-data.html).
These are still natural images, so a network pretrained on ImageNet works directly, with a logistic regressor trained on
top of the last convolutional layer. The average fish is about 180 × 180 pixels, but Keras’ ResNet-50 needs inputs larger
than 197 × 197, so I always upsize the detector’s input to 200 × 200.

- **VGG19.** I started with Keras’ pretrained VGG19; after 50 epochs (~100 seconds) the logistic regression reaches:

  ```text
  32716/32716 [==========] - 2s - loss: 0.0485 - acc: 0.9970 - val_loss: 0.1134 - val_acc: 0.9925
  ```

- **ResNet-50.** As in the blog post above, ResNet’s last pooling layer gives features that generalise better:

  ```text
  32716/32716 [==========] - 1s - loss: 1.1921e-07 - acc: 1.0000 - val_loss: 0.0465 - val_acc: 0.9956
  ```

So ResNet-50 extracts the convolutional features for the rest of the experiments.

**Hard negative mining**, following the HOG + SVM pedestrian-detection pipeline.

**Fine-tuning the convolutional layers.** Next, the ResNet’s convolutional layers are fine-tuned, with data augmentation
added to the binary detector:

```python
ImageDataGenerator(rescale=1.,
                   featurewise_center=True,
                   shear_range=0.2,
                   zoom_range=0.2,
                   rotation_range=360,
                   horizontal_flip=True)
```

Training accuracy is much lower than for the bottleneck network because of the augmentation, and with every
convolutional layer being tuned, training takes considerably longer:

```text
11206/11206 [==========] - 527s - loss: 0.1217 - acc: 0.9580 - val_loss: 0.0295 - val_acc: 0.9898
```

**A fully convolutional detector.** Turning fully connected layers into convolutions was proposed by
[Sermanet et al. 2014](#ref-overfeat) and popularised by [Long et al. 2015](#ref-fcn). Converting the last layer before
pooling (a softmax layer) turns the trained binary fish classifier into a fully convolutional network (FCN), whose
output is a heat map of whether each 56 × 56 grid cell holds a fish.

**Multi-scale detection.** Each output cell covers 56 × 56 pixels and fish vary a lot in size, so I run the FCN over
several scales of the test image. The detector was trained on 200 × 200 inputs with fish of about 180 × 180, so I start
from the original image size and scale it up by 1.1 eight times: nine response maps, from scale 1 to 1.1⁸ ≈ 2.14. As
the [illustrations below](#multiscale) show, for a large fish (top) the smaller-scale maps respond more confidently,
whereas for a small fish (bottom) the upscaled maps respond more strongly at the fish’s location.

<Figure
  id="multiscale"
  src={[multiscaleLarge, multiscaleSmall]}
  alt={[
    'A large fish on deck and its detector response maps at three scales.',
    'A small fish on deck and its detector response maps at three scales.',
  ]}
>
  Response maps for different input sizes, upscaled to the original image size. For a larger fish (top) the smaller-scale
  map gives the stronger confidence; for a smaller fish (bottom) the upscaled maps do.
</Figure>

**Attention map.** Some areas near the image border respond very strongly because parts of the ship resemble the
outline of a fish. I designed a heuristic attention map that damps the response near the left, right and top borders —
but not the bottom, since salient objects can appear at the bottom of the frame but rarely at the top (the sky, for
example). The [figure](#attention-map) shows it cleaning up false positives.

<Figure
  id="attention-map"
  src={[outMean, attentionMap, outMeanAttention, starExample]}
  alt={[

  ]}
>
  From left to right: (1) the original response map; (2) the attention mask; (3) the filtered response map; (4) with the
  original map, the most confident point (red dot) lands near the top of the image; with the attention map, the red star
  gets the higher salience score.
</Figure>

**From response maps to boxes.** Knowing that each image holds only one fish category, I take the maximum response as
the centre of the fish, keep the connected region above a **multiplying threshold**, and expand it by a **factor**. A
grid search using **intersection over union (IoU)** set the threshold to 0.4 and the expansion factor to 1.4. (A side
note: in this IoU the predicted area counts only half, because slightly larger boxes help capture the whole fish — for
fine-grained classification the details matter, and we want each detected fish to be as complete as possible.)

<Figure src={boxes} alt="A detector response map, the selected region as a mask, and the resulting crop of the test image.">
  Response map, selected region and resulting crop for img_03547.
</Figure>

**Evaluating detection.** In **PASCAL VOC**, the highest-confidence box with 50% overlap counts as correct and all others
as wrong; [Hoiem et al. 2012](#ref-hoiem) analyse detection errors in detail. Here I relaxed the criterion so that 30%
overlap counts as a correct detection.

### 2. Fish alignment

I didn’t manage to align the fish the way the “Right Whale” challenge did, partly because the fish vary a lot here and
the head and tail don’t always appear together. (Should we have? Perhaps a network could classify the fish and predict
its head and tail locations at the same time.)

### 3. Fish classification

**Fine-tuning ResNet-50.** Again following
[Building powerful image classification models using very little data](https://blog.keras.io/building-powerful-image-classification-models-using-very-little-data.html),
applying a single network directly to the fish extracted by the detector gave my very first result:

**Clipping.** The metric is cross-entropy, which punishes confident mistakes heavily, so I clip every prediction
([log loss tricks](https://www.kaggle.com/sbrugman/the-nature-conservancy-fisheries-monitoring/tricks-for-the-kaggle-leaderboard/notebook),
[making sense of logarithmic loss](https://web.archive.org/web/20181219165431/http://www.exegetic.biz/blog/2015/12/making-sense-logarithmic-loss/)):

<Formula display tex={String.raw`\mathrm{Pred}' = \operatorname{clip}\left(\mathrm{Pred},\ \frac{1 - m_x}{7},\ m_x\right)`} />

where <Formula tex="m_x" /> is a clipping variable, chosen heuristically as 0.95.

<Figure src={logloss} alt="Log loss falling steeply from about 5 towards 0 as the predicted probability rises from 0 to 1." narrow>
  Log loss against the probability predicted for the true class.
</Figure>

**Averaging over rotations.** I also rotate each test image by 90° four times and average the predictions.

<Figure src={rotation} alt="Four rotations of the same fish crop, labelled with different predicted species and confidences.">
  Rotated copies of an image can give very different results.
</Figure>

<h3 id="day-night">Day and night boats</h3>

Looking at the results, daytime boats with a “normal” colour cast are classified correctly and with high confidence. On
night boats, with their yellowish cast, the classifier tends to put many fish into the yellowfin tuna (YFT) category —
there are too few examples for the network to learn invariance to lighting. So I set out to train separate classifiers
for day and night conditions.

<Figure src={night} alt="Four night-time fish crops with a yellow cast and their predicted labels, mostly yellowfin tuna.">
  Incorrect labels caused by night-time lighting.
</Figure>

## A holistic classifier

Albeit rather brute force, classifying the whole image works surprisingly well — sometimes better than the crops
([discussion: worse performance on cropped images?](https://www.kaggle.com/c/the-nature-conservancy-fisheries-monitoring/discussion/30873)).
I suspect two reasons:

1. [Boat leakage](https://www.kaggle.com/anokas/the-nature-conservancy-fisheries-monitoring/finding-boatids/comments):
   in this dataset the boat is strongly correlated with the kinds of fish it caught.
2. Context: what surrounds the fish is itself a valuable cue for classifying it.

## Ideas I didn’t implement, or couldn’t make work

- [Right whale recognition by deepsense](https://deepsense.io/deep-learning-right-whale-recognition-kaggle/): for
  detection I actually started from their blog post, where whale detection uses the now-popular grid-based pipeline. I
  experimented with their [code](https://www.dropbox.com/s/rohrc1btslxwxzr/deepsense-whales.zip?dl=1) but couldn’t get
  the network to converge on this fishery data.
- [DetectNet](https://devblogs.nvidia.com/parallelforall/detectnet-deep-neural-network-object-detection-digits/): deep
  neural-network object detection in DIGITS, whose data representation is inspired by [Redmon et al. 2015](#ref-yolo).
